Handbook Use & Printing Guide

OVERVIEW

The PCC Handbook supplements TCCC-oriented handbooks such as the Ranger Medic Handbook or the CTM handbook.

Provides Role 1-level guidance on performing basic PCC in austere, low-signature environments without the ability to ventilate patients.

Providers/teams with ventilator capabilities can contact lead authors to obtain the advanced PCC handbook.

PRINTING THE MANUAL

The handbook is designed to be carried in a cargo pocket

It is formatted for the same paper size as the Ranger Medic Handbook (6.5 inches wide x 7.5 inches tall)

If exact paper size unavailable, print two pages on one sheet of letter-sized paper and then fold/cut in half

Printing double-sided can be challenging without access to a dedicated print shop (if in doubt, print double-sided)

If using dedicated print shop, request waterproof paper and binding

If waterproof paper unavailable, place clear plastic or other cover front and back to minimize wear, tear and moisture exposure

If dedicated binding unavailable, can improvise binding with hole punch and string/paracord

The Goal of Triaging: rapidly sort casualties into one of four categories:

Green = Minimal intervention (should face towards most likely enemy threat)

Yellow = unclear prognosis

Red = severely wounded

Black = dead or expectant

Immediate Actions that can be performed during Triage:

Tourniquet placement/checks

Pressure dressing

Chest seal

Needle Decompression/Thoracostomy

Basic Airway clearance

Use the SPEAR-10 and SPEAR-60 algorithms to quickly and efficiently sort casualties into categories:

SPEAR-TEN (ten seconds to complete).   Reserved for worst-case MASCAL. Designed for on-scene MASCAL as well as more rapid throughput if required to optimize number of Immediate Actions.

SPEAR-SIXTY (sixty seconds to complete).   Standard triaging algorithm, sometimes still usable during MASCAL. Designed to optimize sensitivity and specificity for Green and Red casualties (in particular, Green patients are truly Green).

Canthotomy

DEFINITION Release of the lateral canthus to decompress the orbit and reduce intraocular pressure  WHAT THIS PROCEDURE DOES •   Releases pressure within the orbit •   Restores blood flow to retina and optic nerve

WHEN TO CONSIDER •   Suspected retrobulbar hematoma with signs of orbital compartment syndrome:      •   Decreasing vision or visual acuity      •   Proptosis (bulging eye)      •   Severe eye pain or pressure      •   Tense orbit / eyelids      •   Limited extraocular movement

Vision loss + proptosis after trauma → treat immediately

IMPORTANT CONTEXT •   Vision loss may become permanent within hours •   This is a time-critical, limb-equivalent (eye) salvage procedure •   Do not delay for evacuation if clear indication

BOTTOM LINE •   Perform only with clear signs of orbital compartment syndrome •   If vision is deteriorating → act quickly •   If unsure but suspicion high → err on treating

Escharotomy

DEFINITION Release of constricting full-thickness burn eschar to restore circulation  WHAT THIS PROCEDURE DOES •   Relieves pressure from non-compliant burned tissue •   Allows restoration of distal blood flow

WHEN TO CONSIDER •   Circumferential full-thickness burn •   Evidence of vascular compromise:     •   Diminished or absent distal pulses     •   Increasing pain or tightness     •   Distal pallor, coolness, or decreased perfusion

If pulses are present → DO NOT perform

IMPORTANT CONTEXT

Most cases present within first few hours after burn

If no indication within ~24 hours, unlikely to require escharotomy

Decreases pressures but creates open wounds prone to infection

This is a bridge to evacuation, not definitive care

BOTTOM LINE •   Perform only if clearly indicated •   Perform only if evacuation is delayed •   If unsure → do not perform

Escharotomy

BEFORE YOU CUT •   Reassess for other causes of poor perfusion:     •   Shock / hypovolemia     •   Hypotension     •   Associated trauma •   Remove:     •   Tight dressings, splints, or wraps •   Confirm:     •   Burn is full-thickness and circumferential

DEFINITION

Surgical release of fascial compartments to relieve pressure and restore perfusion

WHAT THIS PROCEDURE DOES

Relieves pressure within fascial compartments

Prevents irreversible muscle and nerve damage

WHEN TO CONSIDER

Suspected compartment syndrome with:

Severe pain out of proportion to injury

Pain with passive stretch

Tense, swollen compartment

Progressive neurologic deficit

Late findings (unreliable):

Pallor

Pulselessness

Pulses are present in most cases (do not rely on pulse loss)

Condition may evolve over hours to days

Valkyrie Kits:

KITS SHOULD ONLY BE USED BY PERSONNEL PREVIOUSLY TRAINED ON THEIR USE. DUE TO HIGH RISK FOR DONOR AND RECIPIENT, THE FIELD IS NOT THE PLACE TO LEARN HOW TO CONDUCT TRANSFUSIONS.

Key PCC Considerations for Fresh Whole Transfusion.

Whole blood in the PCC environment is a rare commodity

Obtaining whole blood (temporarily) takes several people out of the fight

Demand for transfusion kits will likely exceed supply

Always control external/extremity bleeding before blood administration

PCC Indications for Fresh Whole Transfusion.   There is only a single hard indicator for transfusion in the austere PCC setting. Due to resource limitations, always consider overall likelihood of evacuation and survival rather than transfusing based on vital signs and injury patterns alone:

SBP <100

PCC Contraindications to Transfusion.   The following are relative contraindications to transfusion and bear a high risk of wasted resources

High suspicion for internal bleeding

High suspicion for major TBI

Ongoing or Impending post-traumatic cardiac arrest

Intramuscular Administration Guidelines.

Choose a site with a large muscle group such as thigh, buttock or deltoid.

Ensure there is no tourniquet proximal to site.

Consider whether hemorrhagic shock will lead to poor absorption from the site.

Hold needle at 90 degrees to skin.

Insert needle deep into muscle.

Aspirate briefly to ensure needle tip is not in a blood vessel.

Adjust depth/placement as needed and re-aspirate.

Inject at a steady rate, about 2mL per second.

1st MARDIV

PROLONGED CASUALTY CARE

96-HOUR AUSTERE / CONTESTED ENVIRONMENT

Time-based MARCHPAWSL assessment | trend recognition | intervention-response reassessment | resource allocation

Educational framework for trained corpsmen

CORNERSTONE 1

THE PCC MINDSET: CTM -> PCC

The mission changes when evacuation is delayed.

COMBAT TRAUMA MANAGEMENT

CONTROL THE IMMEDIATE THREAT

Stop massive hemorrhage

Open and protect the airway

Restore ventilation / treat immediate chest threats

Prevent hypothermia

Prepare for evacuation

TIME HORIZON: seconds to hours

PROLONGED CASUALTY CARE

MAINTAIN LIFE AND PREVENT SECONDARY DEATH

Detect deterioration early

Reassess every intervention

Prevent predictable complications

Manage nursing and supportive care

Allocate blood, oxygen, fluids, medications, power and personnel

TIME HORIZON: minutes to 96+ hours

TCCC controls the immediate threat. PCC manages the consequences of injury over time.

CORNERSTONE 2

THE PCC CLINICAL REASONING CYCLE

A continuous, time-based process - not a one-time checklist.

1 ASSESS

Current physiologic state

2 TREND

Direction of change

3 IDENTIFY

Active / emerging problem

4 INTERVENE

Apply intervention

5 EXPECT

Predict response + time

6 REASSESS

Did it occur?

7 ADAPT

Continue / modify / escalate

KEY PRINCIPLE

Failure to obtain the expected response is itself a clinical finding that requires action.

Assess - trend - intervene - predict - reassess - adapt - then repeat.

CORNERSTONE 3

## 206285: M — Massive Hemorrhage

Archive member: `activities/book_654759/book.xml`

M — Massive Hemorrhage

Massive hemorrhage remains the first priority because early uncontrolled bleeding can kill before many other injuries have time to declare themselves.

TCCC is designed to identify and stop immediately life-threatening hemorrhage. PCC begins by proving that control persists over time, then deliberately looking for bleeding that cannot be seen.

CONTROL VISIBLE BLEEDING → CONFIRM CONTROL → SEARCH FOR NONVISIBLE BLEEDING → TREND SHOCK → CORRECT PHYSIOLOGIC FAILURE → REASSESS / RE-TRIAGE

VISIBLE / COMPRESSIBLE

Tourniquets, packed wounds, pressure dressings, junctional control and pelvic stabilization can fail, migrate, loosen, saturate or become ineffective after movement and time.

NONVISIBLE / OCCULT

Thoracic, abdominal, retroperitoneal, pelvic and incompletely controlled vascular bleeding may be recognized primarily through changing physiology and trends.

Key PCC distinction: temporized does not mean definitively controlled. A casualty who looked stable earlier may deteriorate from rebleeding, occult hemorrhage, loss of clot stability, or lethal-diamond physiology.

Massive Hemorrhage — Core Teaching & 96-Hour PCC

Why hemorrhage still matters in PCC

Early recognition and control of life-threatening hemorrhage are essential for survival. TCCC improves early survival through rapid external hemorrhage control, resuscitation, and evacuation. When evacuation is delayed or denied, the medical team must assume that initial control may be incomplete, temporary, or vulnerable to failure.

TCCC → PCC transition

TCCC emphasisPCC extension

Find and stop immediately life-threatening external bleeding.Reassess every prior control measure and prove that hemostasis persists.

Rapid movement toward evacuation and definitive care.Manage hours-to-days of device maintenance, rebleeding risk, occult loss, physiologic deterioration, and resource depletion.

Initial shock recognition and resuscitation.Trend response over time and repeatedly ask whether poor perfusion means failed control, nonvisible bleeding, coagulopathy, or more than one process.

Why tourniquet time matters in the 1MARDIV PCC model: after a missile-strike MASCAL, one Corpsman may be responsible for several casualties at once. The provider must intentionally track unresolved tourniquets while continuing MARCH care on everyone else. The goal is to begin reassessment early, convert eligible tourniquets within 2 hours when possible, and avoid allowing an unattended tourniquet to drift past the 4-hour routine field-conversion ceiling.

Tourniquet conversion is a time-dependent PCC decision

Tourniquet conversion should begin with early reassessment, not with a plan to wait for a fixed clock time. Current JTS/TCCC guidance supports conversion as soon as possible when the casualty is not in shock, the wound can be closely monitored, and the tourniquet is not controlling bleeding from an amputated extremity. Every effort should be made to convert an eligible tourniquet in less than 2 hours.

1MARDIV PCC operational construct: in a missile-strike mass-casualty event, one Corpsman may be cycling among several critically injured casualties. The course therefore treats 4 hours as the operational ceiling for routine field conversion planning—not as the preferred time to wait and not as an instruction to remove every tourniquet at 4 hours.

Think about ischemic tissue burden

The metabolic burden of prolonged limb ischemia is not identical for every tourniquet. In general, longer ischemic duration and a larger mass of ischemic skeletal muscle may increase the potential reperfusion burden. A very proximal lower-extremity occlusion can involve substantially more muscle mass than a more distal occlusion; a lower extremity generally contains more muscle mass than an upper extremity.

When blood flow is restored after prolonged ischemia, potassium, acids/lactate, myoglobin and other intracellular/metabolic products can return to the circulation. Associated blast/crush injury, direct muscle trauma, shock, temperature, renal function, and the duration of ischemia may all modify the risk.

Important: this tissue-burden concept is for risk assessment; it is not an instruction to move a correctly placed life-saving tourniquet distally or to remove a tourniquet before safe alternative hemorrhage control is established.

Teaching emphasis: “4 hours” is a ceiling for routine field-conversion planning in the 1MARDIV PCC MASCAL model. The desired behavior is early reassessment, conversion within 2 hours when eligible, deliberate tracking from 2–4 hours, and recognition that ≥4 hours is a different reperfusion-risk problem requiring greater capability.

Nonvisible Hemorrhage — Search for Occult Loss

Visible hemorrhage controlled does not mean hemorrhage controlled.

After external control is verified, unexplained or worsening shock must trigger a deliberate search for nonvisible bleeding while other causes of deterioration are considered in parallel.

Where can the blood be going?

THORAX

Ongoing intrathoracic bleeding may declare itself through increasing chest-drain output, worsening respiratory status, declining perfusion or poor resuscitation response.

ABDOMEN

Intra-abdominal bleeding may remain clinically subtle until compensatory mechanisms fail. Reassess mechanism, abdominal findings and shock trend.

RETROPERITONEUM

Blood loss may be large and externally invisible. Mechanism, associated injury and a worsening perfusion trend may be the principal clues.

PELVIS

Suspect with compatible mechanism, pelvic pain/instability, major lower-extremity injury or unexplained shock. Verify binder position; avoid repeated manipulation.

Noncompressible torso hemorrhage surveillance

Reassess penetrating and blunt chest/torso injuries after movement and whenever physiology changes.

Trend chest-tube drainage volume, character and rate when a tube is present.

Trend mental status, pulse quality, heart rate, blood pressure, skin perfusion, respiratory status and response to blood/resuscitation.

Use mechanism and associated injury to keep pelvic and retroperitoneal bleeding on the differential even when there is no external blood.

Recognize that PCC measures may only temporize noncompressible hemorrhage; definitive hemostasis often requires surgical capability.

Compensated shock can hide hemorrhage

Shock is a pattern, not one number. A casualty may maintain near-normal blood pressure while becoming progressively tachycardic, cool, pale, confused, nauseated or poorly perfused. Serial trends are more useful than a single snapshot.

VISIBLE CONTROL CONFIRMED + SHOCK PERSISTS → SEARCH THORAX / ABDOMEN / RETROPERITONEUM / PELVIS → TREND RESPONSE → ESCALATE EVACUATION / SURGICAL NEED

Hemorrhagic Shock & the Lethal Diamond

Shock is a pattern, not a number

PatternWhat the team may seePCC implication

CompensatedVital signs may appear deceptively acceptable while peripheral perfusion changes.Keep trending; do not assume durable hemostasis.

DecompensatingWorsening tachycardia, weaker pulses, cool/clammy skin, declining perfusion, sometimes altered mental status.Recheck visible control, search occult sources, assess resuscitation response and re-triage.

The lethal diamond reopens the bleeding problem

Hypothermia

Impairs coagulation and platelet function.

Acidosis

Reflects inadequate perfusion and reduces coagulation efficiency.

Hypocalcemia

Impairs cardiac performance and coagulation during resuscitation.

Coagulopathy

Destabilizes clot formation and promotes ongoing or recurrent bleeding.

Correcting lethal-diamond physiology is part of hemorrhage control. A mechanically controlled wound can rebleed when physiology deteriorates.

Prolonged tourniquet release: anticipate reperfusion physiology

After prolonged limb ischemia, release may return potassium, acid load, myoglobin and other metabolites to the central circulation. Risk is influenced by ischemic duration, the amount of ischemic muscle, associated muscle injury/crush, shock and renal reserve. A proximal lower-extremity tourniquet may therefore present a different physiologic burden than a smaller distal ischemic segment.

PCC implication: once tourniquet time exceeds the routine conversion window, release is no longer simply a wound-care task. It becomes a monitored resuscitation decision with potential hyperkalemia/reperfusion consequences.

Every resuscitation creates a prediction

After hemorrhage control and blood/resuscitation, define what should improve: mental status, pulse quality, perfusion, heart rate/blood-pressure trend and overall clinical trajectory. If the expected response does not occur, do not simply repeat the same intervention indefinitely.

INTERVENE → EXPECT IMPROVED PERFUSION → REASSESS → IF RESPONSE IS POOR: RECHECK CONTROL + SEARCH OCCULT BLEEDING + CORRECT PHYSIOLOGY + RE-TRIAGE

Resource stewardship

Blood products, calcium, warming capability, TXA when indicated, monitoring, manpower and evacuation capacity are finite. Persistent hemorrhage changes not only the casualty's physiology but also the resource posture for every other casualty being held.

Five ways hemorrhage control fails in PCC

Mechanical

Tourniquet failure, dressing loosening/saturation, packing failure, binder migration or device displacement.

Physiologic

Ongoing internal bleeding, progressive shock, coagulopathy, hypothermia, acidosis and hypocalcemia.

Detection

Slow drift into shock is missed because one measurement looked acceptable.

Cognitive / operational

Fatigue, multiple casualties, competing tasks and limited manpower reduce reliable reassessment.

Temporal

Occult bleeding declares itself, clot stability is lost or physiologic reserve becomes exhausted over time.

The closing model

Troubleshoot prior TCCC control. Correct mechanical failure first.

Move from visible to nonvisible. Once external bleeding is controlled, deliberately search thoracic, abdominal, retroperitoneal and pelvic sources.

Anticipate deterioration. Shock and lethal-diamond physiology can destabilize clots and reveal occult loss.

Re-triage. Persistent or recurrent hemorrhage changes evacuation priority, blood allocation, manpower demand and likely survivability.

FOUNDATION  ·  01 / 05

Shock is a delivery–utilization failure

Circulatory shock is inadequate oxygen delivery to tissues, inadequate cellular oxygen use, or both — not a blood pressure number.

DELIVERY (DO₂)

Oxygen delivered = pump × oxygen content

Cardiac output  —  heart rate × stroke volume (preload, contractility, afterload).

Arterial O₂ content  —  hemoglobin × saturation, plus a small dissolved fraction.

Drop either side and tissues run short — bleeding, pump failure, hypoxemia, anemia.

UTILIZATION & THE LACTATE SIGNAL

Cells must extract O₂ and make ATP — or fail.

Extraction climbs as delivery falls — until a critical floor.

Below that, mitochondria switch to anaerobic ATP; lactate and base deficit rise.

Persistent lactate or worsening base deficit = ongoing tissue oxygen debt, even if BP looks acceptable.

Source: Shock — StatPearls (NCBI) · Circulatory shock — an update · Surviving Sepsis Campaign 2021

PERSISTENCE  ·  04 / 05

Why shock persists after volume repletion

Volume alone fixes one piece of one mechanism. Rust = lethal-triad / ongoing-source mechanisms — fluids will not fix these.

Vasoplegia

Inflamed vessels won’t constrict; tone collapses despite fluids and norepinephrine.

Myocardial depression

Cytokine- and ischemia-driven pump failure; output stays low at any preload.

Acidosis

Tissue hypoxia drives lactic acid; pressors and inotropes lose effect.

Hypothermia

Cold slows enzymes and clotting; part of the lethal triad in trauma.

Coagulopathy

Trauma-induced — worsened by dilution from fluids, hypothermia, and acidosis.

Mitochondrial failure

Cells can’t use oxygen even when delivery is restored — cytopathic hypoxia.

Inflammatory injury

Cytokine storm, glycocalyx shedding, capillary leak — ongoing endothelial damage.

Ongoing source / bleed

Untreated infection or bleeding refills the deficit faster than you can resuscitate.

Corpsman reassessment — beyond “is the tank full?”

Walk the chain every reassessment. If the patient is not improving, ask which link you have not addressed.

PUMP

Is the heart moving blood?  Rate and rhythm; obstructive causes (tension pneumo, tamponade, PE); does the patient need an inotrope, not more fluid?

PIPES

Is vascular tone holding?  MAP target ~65 on pressors; norepinephrine first-line; consider vasoplegia if BP fails despite volume.

CAPILLARY DELIVERY

CELLULAR USE

Are cells using the oxygen?  Persistent lactate / base deficit despite restored macro = micro/mitochondrial problem.

TRIAD & SOURCE

Have you broken the lethal triad?  Warm the patient, limit crystalloid, give blood products, stop the bleed or treat the source.

## 206333: Logistics / Life Support — 96-Hour PCC Sustainment

Archive member: `activities/book_654759/book.xml`

Logistics / Life Support — 96-Hour PCC Sustainment

During prolonged casualty care, logistics becomes a clinical intervention. A treatment plan that cannot be sustained is not a complete treatment plan.

Operating question: Can this team sustain this casualty, this treatment plan, and itself until the next realistic evacuation or resupply opportunity?

Forecast instead of inventory

Do not stop at “what do we have?” Estimate what will remain at 12, 24, 48, 72 and 96 hours. Recalculate after every new casualty, major intervention, resupply delay, or change in evacuation estimate.

ResourceForecastClinical consequence if depleted

Blood / fluidsCurrent stock, expected use, warming/storage capability, likely resupplyReduced ability to resuscitate new or deteriorating casualties.

OxygenSupply, flow/use rate, device demand, concentrator/power dependenceMay force changes in respiratory support and evacuation priority.

MedicationsAntibiotics, analgesia/sedation, emergency drugs, scheduled dosing over timeMissed therapy, inability to sustain advanced airway or symptom control.

Dressings / procedure suppliesWound-care cycle, chest/airway/IV supplies, PPE, cleaning materialsLoss of wound, device or infection-control capability.

Power / batteriesDevice burn rate, charging capacity, generator dependence, spare batteriesLoss of monitoring, suction, warming, ventilation or communications.

Water / food / hygieneCasualty and team requirements, sanitation and waste burdenDehydration, fatigue, infection risk and reduced team performance.

PersonnelSkill mix, watch rotation, nursing workload, sleep and physical demandErrors, missed reassessment and inability to sustain labor-intensive care.

Green / Yellow / Red resource status

GREEN: adequate for the anticipated interval. YELLOW: projected limitation requires conservation, substitution, redistribution or resupply planning. RED: current or imminent shortage threatens casualty care or team survival and requires command/evacuation prioritization.

Resupply and evacuation assumptions

Identify the earliest realistic resupply and evacuation windows.

Identify items that may not be resupplied at all.

Plan for interdicted routes, delayed movement, degraded communications and changing casualty load.

Prepare casualties for transfer before the window opens; do not wait for transport to arrive before organizing records, medications, devices and handoff.

FORECAST → ALLOCATE → CONSERVE → SUBSTITUTE → REASSESS → RESUPPLY / RE-TRIAGE

## 206334: Logistics / Life Support — Contested Sustainment & Signature Cost

Archive member: `activities/book_654759/book.xml`

Logistics / Life Support — Contested Sustainment & Signature Cost

In a contested environment, every additional capability has a sustainment cost. Power, oxygen, refrigeration, communications, waste, movement and resupply can also increase the observable footprint of a medical position.

Capability versus burden

CapabilityWhat it addsWhat must be planned

Powered medical equipmentMonitoring, ventilation, suction, warming, diagnosticsBatteries, charging, heat/noise/light, backup method and failure plan.

Oxygen systemsRespiratory supportWeight, cylinder duration or concentrator power demand, movement and resupply.

Cold-chain / warmingBlood and medication supportPower or passive alternatives, insulation, duration and contingency.

Communications / telemedicineConsultation, coordination and evacuation planningUnit communications/EMCON plan, power, transmission opportunity and fallback documentation.

Repeated resupplyRestores consumablesMovement, manpower, route availability, timing and operational exposure.

Pre-position for the hold

When tactically feasible, pre-positioning the resources required for the anticipated PCC window reduces dependence on repeated resupply. The goal is not maximal equipment; it is the minimum sustainable capability that meets the clinical mission with a viable contingency.

Provider life support

Provider sustainment is part of patient care. Build a watch plan that protects sleep, hydration, nutrition, hygiene, physical recovery, documentation and handoff. Fatigue converts directly into missed trends, medication errors, poor procedures and degraded tactical awareness.

Displacement: what moves with the casualty?

Life-sustaining interventions and immediate rescue equipment

Critical medications and next-dose requirements

Documentation and trend data

Power / oxygen required for the movement interval plus contingency

Secure tubing, drains, lines and devices before movement

Define what can be cached, abandoned, substituted or transferred

Contested-care principle: the best clinical device is not automatically the best operational choice. Consider benefit, weight, power, manpower, resupply dependence, mobility and signature together.

## 206175: 1. Survive to Treat — Battlefield Medical Provider Survivability

Archive member: `activities/book_653971/book.xml`

1. Survive to Treat — Battlefield Medical Provider Survivability

Provider survivability is medical capability. A Corpsman, medic, or medical team that is detected, fixed, isolated, exhausted, or rendered nonfunctional loses the ability to preserve future casualties.

Course proposition: Preserve the provider → preserve the medical team → preserve treatment capacity → preserve combat power.

Why this matters

Persistent surveillance, precision fires, degraded communications, contested evacuation, and prolonged holding force medical personnel to balance optimal care with the ability to survive, move, conceal, sustain themselves, and remain clinically functional.

Learning objectives

Explain why survivability is a prerequisite for prolonged casualty care.

Recognize how routine medical activity can create observable signatures.

Balance casualty benefit, provider/team risk, resource burden, and mission requirements.

Use repeated reassessment rather than a one-time “hide the CCP” mindset.

Two questions at every decision

Clinical:
What does this casualty need now, and what response do I expect?

Survivability:
What signature, sustainment burden, or exposure does that action create for the team?

2. Understanding the Medical Signature

A medical position does not create one signature. It creates a changing signature pattern across multiple domains.

Teaching model: ACTIVITY → SIGNATURE → DETECTION OPPORTUNITY → THREAT → CONSEQUENCE → ADAPTATION

Four major domains

Visual
Light, contrast, movement, disturbed terrain, clustering, waste, repeated routes.

Thermal
People, warming systems, engines, power sources, electronics, concentrated activity.

Acoustic
Voices, alarms, motors, generators, vehicles, repeated movement.

RF / electromagnetic
Radios, wireless-enabled devices, data links, telemedicine, networked equipment.

Signature convergence

A single observation may be ambiguous. Multiple signatures that correlate in location and time can increase confidence that an activity or position is present. PCC increases this problem because treatment, observation, power, communications, logistics, and casualty movement continue for hours to days.

Reassessment

AskMeaning

What changed?New casualty, device, transmission, resupply, movement, light, heat, noise, or waste?

What accumulated?Repeated routes, prolonged occupation, resource depletion, personnel clustering?

What can be reduced without unacceptable clinical harm?Prioritize clinically necessary capability while reducing avoidable exposure.

## 206178: 3. The Low-Signature Medical Position

Archive member: `activities/book_653971/book.xml`

3. The Low-Signature Medical Position

The goal is not an “invisible” medical site. The goal is a clinically effective, sustainable position whose avoidable signatures are deliberately managed.

Position design considerations

Use terrain, existing structures, concealment, dispersion, and access control consistent with the supported unit's plan.

Plan patient flow, treatment areas, power, communications, waste, resupply, and evacuation together rather than as separate problems.

Avoid unnecessary concentration of people, equipment, heat, light, noise, and electronic emissions.

Maintain the ability to examine casualties, perform essential interventions, and displace when required.

Clinical necessity test

QuestionDecision purpose

Is this capability essential to an achievable clinical outcome here?Prevents equipment accumulation without patient benefit.

What power, resupply, movement, communication, and staffing does it require?Exposes the true sustainment cost.

Is a simpler or passive option clinically acceptable?May preserve capability while reducing burden.

What happens if it fails or must be abandoned?Forces a contingency plan before deterioration.

Do not sacrifice essential care simply to reduce signature. Signature management is a risk-balancing discipline, not a substitute for clinical judgment.

## 206179: 4. The Corpsman / Medic as a Survivor

4. The Corpsman / Medic as a Survivor

Clinical excellence cannot be applied if the provider cannot function in the environment long enough to reach, treat, sustain, and move casualties.

SURVIVE → REMAIN FUNCTIONAL → PRESERVE MEDICAL CAPABILITY → TREAT → MOVE → SURVIVE AGAIN

The tactical foundation

The Warrior Medic framework describes a progression beginning with baseline field survivability and developing toward providers who can integrate medical support with maneuver, terrain, logistics, commander’s intent, and anticipated casualty-producing events.

Baseline survivor
Endurance, fieldcraft, navigation, concealment, minimal footprint, self-sustainment.

Defensive combat provider
Protects patients and teammates when danger cannot be avoided.

Expeditionary tactical provider
Integrates medical support with the supported formation and scheme of maneuver.

Advanced tactical provider
Balances advanced clinical capability with mature tactical judgment in highly contested conditions.

Readiness is perishable

Survivability should be trained, assessed, remediated, and sustained rather than treated as a one-time schoolhouse event. The medical load, physical conditioning, personal sustainment, communications proficiency, fieldcraft, and clinical skills must work together.

1. Tracking Fundamentals — Reading the Ground and Environment

Tracking is the disciplined observation and interpretation of changes in the environment.

Core idea: Do not hunt only for a perfect footprint. Establish what “undisturbed” looks like, then identify changes and combine multiple indicators before drawing a conclusion.

1.1 What is sign?

Ground sign
Compression, scuffs, displaced soil, disturbed leaves, altered rocks, impressions.

High sign
Vegetation or objects disturbed above ground level.

Temporary sign
Changes that fade relatively quickly as weather and the environment recover.

Persistent sign
Changes that remain longer and can continue to indicate prior activity.

1.2 Six traditional indicator categories

Displacement — something has moved from its normal position.

Staining / transfer — material has moved from one surface to another.

Weathering — sun, wind, moisture and precipitation change sign over time.

Odor — people, equipment, food, fuel, waste and occupied positions may produce detectable odor.

Litter — lost or discarded material can indicate presence and activity.

Immediate-use information — indicators become useful when combined into a coherent, time-sensitive picture.

1.3 Observation before interpretation

Separate what you see from what you think it means. A disciplined tracker records the observation first, then compares it with other indicators before assigning meaning.

PERFORMANCE STANDARD: The Marine distinguishes observation from interpretation and uses multiple indicators before reaching a conclusion.

1.4 Build the picture

Ask: What changed? Where does the pattern continue? What direction is supported by multiple indicators? What does terrain or weather do to confidence? Is there evidence of a halt, load, change in pace, or deliberate signature reduction?

2. Countertracking / Antitracking — Managing the Infantry Signature

Countertracking begins by understanding what a tracker or sensor can observe, then deliberately reducing unnecessary signature without sacrificing mission accomplishment.

Countertracking is not “erasing every track.” The practical objective is to reduce the clarity, continuity and usefulness of the signature while maintaining security, tempo and control.

2.1 Think like the observer

Before movement, identify the signatures the unit is most likely to create: disturbed ground and vegetation, visual contrast and movement, light/noise, litter and occupied-site evidence, heat, and electromagnetic emissions.

2.2 Physical signature discipline

Route and terrain
Recognize that different surfaces record disturbance differently. Avoid unnecessary concentration of movement and repeated disturbance.

Halts and positions
Control litter, unnecessary vegetation damage, obvious equipment impressions and repeated movement around a site.

Crossings and transitions
Expect changes in surface, moisture and vegetation to make disturbance easier to recognize.

Team discipline
Signature management is collective. One careless Marine can reveal the movement or occupation pattern of the group.

2.3 Countertracking tradeoffs

Reducing signature costs time, attention and sometimes mobility. Leaders must balance concealment with tempo, security, casualty status, terrain, mission requirements and the threat. A technique that slows a unit excessively can create a different vulnerability.

2.4 Avoid creating a pattern

Repeated behaviors can become signatures even when individual traces are subtle. Consider predictable halts, repeated paths, concentrations, light/noise routines and recurring emissions. Countertracking therefore includes pattern discipline, not only physical camouflage.

2.5 Site departure check

STOP → LOOK BACK → IDENTIFY WHAT CHANGED → REMOVE UNNECESSARY MATERIAL → REDUCE OBVIOUS PATTERN → MOVE

This is a training framework, not a promise of invisibility. A capable observer may still detect the element.

3. Modern Tracking — UAS, Thermal, Electronic & Multispectral Detection

The modern track is a signature. Physical sign now exists beside aerial observation, thermal contrast and electromagnetic emissions.

3.1 UAS and visual detection

Small unmanned aircraft change the geometry of observation. Concealment that works from the ground may not work from above. Movement, contrast, linear patterns, disturbed sites and concentrations can become more apparent from an aerial perspective.

3.2 Thermal signature

Thermal sensors detect differences in emitted heat rather than visible color. The objective of thermal signature management is not to assume a person or formation can become “invisible,” but to understand how heat, movement, background, duration and equipment can change detectability.

Training point: evaluate camouflage and positions from the sensor’s perspective. Daylight visual concealment and nighttime thermal concealment are not the same problem.

3.3 Electromagnetic signature

Radios and other electronic systems can create detectable electromagnetic signatures. Current U.S. Army writing emphasizes understanding what the formation emits, reducing unnecessary emissions, and treating the electromagnetic spectrum as part of survivability rather than as a communications-only concern.

3.4 Multispectral thinking

A measure that improves one signature can worsen another. More equipment can add power demand, heat, bulk or emissions. More movement to improve visual concealment may increase physical disturbance. The leader therefore asks:

WHO CAN DETECT US? → IN WHICH SPECTRUM? → WHAT ARE WE EMITTING OR DISTURBING? → WHAT CAN WE REDUCE WITHOUT LOSING MISSION CAPABILITY?

3.5 Contemporary Army lesson

Recent Army professional writing repeatedly frames survivability as management of visual, thermal and radio/electromagnetic signatures. The light infantry advantage is the ability to operate with a smaller observable footprint—but only if disciplined fieldcraft and emissions control are deliberately trained.

4. Integrated Infantry Practical — Track, Countertrack, Detect & Reassess

Moodle prepares the eye and the decision process. Field training develops the skill.

4.1 Exercise purpose

Train Marines to identify physical sign, interpret it cautiously, recognize their own signatures, and apply defensive signature-management measures while preserving security and mission tempo.

4.2 Lane design

Phase 1 — Baseline
Students inspect undisturbed terrain and describe what “normal” looks like.

Phase 2 — Track
A training element crosses the lane. Students identify observations and distinguish them from interpretations.

Phase 3 — Countertrack
The moving element repeats a comparable problem while deliberately reducing unnecessary physical and observable signature.

Phase 4 — Sensor review
Where authorized training systems are available, compare ground observation with overhead/thermal or emissions-awareness observations.

4.3 Student reporting format

OBSERVE: What did I actually see or detect?

INTERPRET: What are the plausible explanations?

CONFIDENCE: What other indicators support or contradict the interpretation?

IMPLICATION: What does this mean for the patrol or position?

ACTION: What signature can be reduced without unacceptable mission cost?

4.4 AAR questions

Which signatures were easiest to detect?

Which signatures persisted longest?

What did the tracking team infer correctly? What did it infer incorrectly?

Which countermeasures reduced signature, and what did they cost in time, mobility or workload?

Did the element solve one signature problem while creating another?

What should become unit SOP?

PERFORMANCE STANDARD: RECOGNIZE → INTERPRET → REDUCE → REASSESS. The Marine identifies multiple signature types, separates observation from interpretation, applies appropriate defensive signature discipline, and explains the operational tradeoff.

Instructor note

Keep the exercise focused on observation, defensive fieldcraft and signature awareness. Do not reward theatrical “tricks.” Reward disciplined baseline observation, accurate reporting, team signature control and thoughtful tradeoff decisions.
